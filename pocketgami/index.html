Pocketgami

Channelling nervousness into origami, one crease at a time.

A pocket-sized origami ritual designed for one socially anxious student.

Design for One NID Gandhinagar, 2025 Team of 6, six disciplines

The project at a glance

Brief

Design for one real person on campus, not for the masses.

Problem

Subject B’s trust issues create a major barrier in how he communicates with people.

Insight

Anxiety makes him fidget, but he finds calm in slow, familiar, predictable things.

Design intent

Turn anxious, repetitive motion into a grounding ritual with a tactile, familiar object.

Outcome

Pocketgami: tearable origami sheets with crease patterns and a holder for finished folds. Rated 8/10 by him, then validated in a real social setting.

IntroductionBrief

Design for One asked us to find one real person at NID Gandhinagar and design only for them: their habits, quirks and needs, understood in depth.

02

ResearchProblem

Open conversations to collect personal problems and get out of our own bubble. Among them: a light sleeper, someone distressed by loud sounds, a board-game host with no way to carry games, and someone whose trust issues created a major communication barrier.

That last one stayed with us, but it was still too broad to design for.

50–60 minute interviews on trust and communication, asking “why” until we reached something designable. Questions like: who is a good communicator in your life, who do you reach out to in distress, and how?

Patterns sharpened, but we needed more depth per person.

62 questions, 70 minutes each, across themes like trust, non-verbal digital cues, vulnerability and silence.

Affinity mapping failed us here: it surfaced what everyone shared, the opposite of what Design for One needs. So we flipped it and looked for outliers within each person.

Our one: Subject B

Why him

  • Ambiverted, so we could see communication from both sides.
  • Rarely uses visuals; reads emojis in ways that aren’t typical of Gen Z.
  • Little feel for tone or punctuation in text.
  • Believes the length of a message is proportional to the problem.
  • Keeps notifications off and replies only when ready. Ragging at his previous college changed how he communicates.

Problem statement

Subject B’s trust issues lead to a major communication barrier with people.

Research question

How can we help an anxious user who fears social judgment engage with others while keeping a sense of control?

From six ideas to four prototypesPrototypes

Six initial concepts led to four prototypes. He used each freely while we watched, then rated it out of 10.

His ratings

Abandoned

Wirestorm

Thread and shape wire through holes in a desk cylinder, and keep the shapes inside.

“Fun and keeps me occupied. The size could be smaller.”

7/10
Mid fidelity

Spiral-O-Graph

A figure-eight of beads on stretchable string, rolled and stretched between two fingers.

“Helps me focus on others while fidgeting.”

6.5/10

Too flashy and slightly bulky; it drew attention.

Selected

Pocketgami

Tearable origami sheets with printed crease patterns, plus a compartment for finished folds.

“At first I felt tense, but as I continued, I could channel my restlessness and focus.”

8/10

Suggestion: a slimmer sleeve and flatter tessellations.

Mid fidelity

Fold Forever

A durable triangular tessellation that folds endlessly, with soft hinges and hard panels.

“I don’t have to focus on fidgeting. It feels like origami.”

8.8/10

Suggestion: elastic joints for more play.

06

Final designOutcome

Pocketgami

A notepad of tearable origami sheets. Blank on the front; a tessellation crease pattern and a calming line on the back. The holder keeps finished folds, so a nervous moment leaves behind something he made.

Why it fits him

Paper, his top materialFolding, his top motionPocket-sizedLooks like a notepadFolds are collectable

The ritual

Try a few folds

Drag or tap across the sheet. This slow, repeatable motion is what the whole project is built on.

0 of 64

Validation

We placed him among unfamiliar people, a setting that made him visibly nervous, and handed him Pocketgami. As he folded, his attention moved from the room to his hands, and the anxiety turned into concentration and a sense of accomplishment. Because it looks like a notepad, he used it without drawing attention.

07

ConclusionReflection

He didn’t need to be changed. He needed to be understood.

His fidgeting wasn’t a distraction but his own way of finding control. Designing for behaviour change means guiding, not forcing.

What we’d do differently

A smaller, sturdier holder, better paper, and sustainable materials.

Where it could go next+
Sheets that grow in complexity so the ritual also teaches. Textured papers. Interchangeable pattern themes and palettes. Trading finished folds as a gentle, low-pressure way to connect with others.
