Let’s begin by understanding wellness tourism in India

Wellness tourism in India refers to travel where the primary goal is to improve physical, mental, and spiritual well-being through indigenous practices and nature-based experiences.

So naturally, the next step was to ask which system was already working?

Wellness tourismSubjective improvementMedical tourismObjective, clinically validated outcomes

We looked at medical tourism… and that’s where it became clear that its structured, trust-driven approach makes the journey very clear… whereas in wellness tourism in India, that customer journey is still missing.

To understand it further, we mapped the Global wellness standards across different countries.

What emerged was striking: despite having a wellness market exceeding $15 billion, India is one of the only countries in this comparison that lacks a mandatory national accreditation system, a structured pre-visit health consultation, and a unified digital health record for international users.

This reveals that while the offerings are strong, the system supporting the user journey remains fragmented, making it difficult for users to discover, trust, and confidently make decisions.

From primary research we understood, the problem lies in Discovery & decision making

Phases of Discovery and Decision making

India is frequently compared against Bali, Thailand, Sri Lanka. Red cards: international tourists. Yellow cards: within India.

Major gaps

  1. Discovery phase gap – scattered information around the wellness industry
  2. Clarity phase gap – no standardisation of services or terminology
  3. Trust phase gap – weak certification visibility and safety signalling
  4. Personalisation phase gap – no diagnosis before treatment purchase
  5. Systemic gap – fragmented, non-integrated user journey

Referrals bypass the research phase, so users arrive with fewer questions asked, making transparency failures more jarring when discovered on-site.

Lack of transparency. Hidden costs and undisclosed rules that emerge on-site damage trust in the centre’s digital presence retroactively. Users feel the website was a curated front, not a truthful preview. When on-ground reality (tagging rules, hidden fees, protocols) diverges from the polished pre-visit impression, the perception of deception is amplified.
Validation from multiple sources. YouTube and Instagram content is treated as an independent evidence layer. Users cross-reference visual cues against review text to build a composite picture of trustworthiness.
Word of mouth. Even after multichannel research, final trust is anchored to a human referrer: the gut feeling that closes the decision loop is almost always social, not informational. A trusted referrer’s endorsement substitutes for formal credential verification.
No accreditation check. Centre websites do not surface AYUSH/NABH status prominently, or at all, and users do not know to look for it, so the website visit reinforces ignorance of formal credentials.

Outcome measurement tool

Measure vikriti, not only prakriti

  • Different questionnaires → different results for the same person. They are clinically meaningful but methodologically weak and inconsistently implemented.
  • No clear separation between prakriti and vikriti. The two are treated as one.
  • Self-perception and vague terms (“Do you have good digestion?”, “often”, “sometimes”).
  • Limited scientific validation. Few tools tested for reliability, construct validity, or correlation with biomarkers.
  • Two practitioners may assess the same person differently.

Prakriti

An individual’s innate, unchanging natural constitution (genetic makeup).

Vikriti

The current state of imbalance or illness.

Our move: measure vikriti from the patient’s side to give outcome evidence.
